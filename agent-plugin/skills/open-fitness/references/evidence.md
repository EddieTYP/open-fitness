# Open Fitness evidence baseline

Last reviewed: 2026-08-23

This is a small population-level baseline, not the owner's personal prescription.
Apply sources in this order:

1. The owner's current stored records and latest stored calorie, protein, and
   training rules.
2. Repeated comparable trends in their workouts, recovery, body measurements,
   Active Energy, and nutrition records.
3. The evidence baseline below, limited to the population it describes.
4. General model knowledge, only for explanation and never to override 1–3.

## Training

### 2026 ACSM resistance training

The 2026 American College of Sports Medicine position stand is the default
resistance-training reference for healthy adults. Its practical baseline is to
train all major muscle groups at least twice weekly and progress gradually.
Consistency and adherence matter more than unnecessary complexity. Match the
prescription to the goal: heavier loading (about 80% 1RM) for strength,
approximately 10 weekly sets per muscle group for hypertrophy, and moderate
loads moved quickly for power. These are starting points; the owner's demonstrated
performance, pain, recovery, schedule, and stated goal decide the recommendation.

The 2026 stand supports individualization and simple, sustainable programming;
it does not prescribe Open Fitness's exact automatic load-increase trigger.

### Load progression

The older ACSM 2009 position stand recommended increasing load by 2–10% when a
person exceeds the target by 1–2 repetitions on two consecutive workouts. Use
that only as the named legacy repetition fallback when reliable RIR is absent.
Open Fitness's RIR-aware two-session gate, confirmation-before-write rule, and
post-deload return to the last normal anchor are conservative product policies,
not quoted ACSM formulas.

Sources:

- https://acsm.org/science-spotlight-acsm-releases-new-position-stand-on-resistance-training/
- https://acsm.org/resistance-training-guidelines-update-2026/
- https://www.acsm.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/Progression-Models-in-Resistance-Training-for-Healthy-Adults.pdf

### WHO physical-activity baseline

Use the WHO 2020 physical-activity and sedentary-behaviour guideline only as a
general health floor for adults, not as a strength or hypertrophy program. Do
not turn a population minimum into a personalized target without checking
the owner's current training load and recovery.

Source: https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240014886

## Nutrition

### ISSN protein and exercise

For healthy exercising adults, the ISSN position stand gives 1.4–2.0 g protein
per kg body weight per day as a general range sufficient for most people, with
0.25 g/kg or roughly 20–40 g as a general per-serving reference. Total daily
intake, energy balance, training, body weight, age, and food quality matter more
than treating one number as universal. The owner's latest stored target remains
the working target. Medical or renal considerations require an appropriate
professional rather than extrapolation from this healthy-population reference.

Source: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12970-017-0177-8

### Hong Kong balanced-diet baseline

Use the Hong Kong Centre for Health Protection guidance as a broad food-quality
check: dietary variety across the main food groups, with attention to excess
fat, salt, and sugar and to micronutrient adequacy. Do not replace the owner's
recorded portions, nutrient labels, or stored calorie budget with food-pyramid
assumptions.

Source: https://www.chp.gov.hk/en/healthtopics/content/25/8800.html

## Safety and attribution

- Advice is coaching, not diagnosis or treatment.
- Pain, symptoms, medication, a diagnosed condition, or a clinically unusual
  trend can make these healthy-adult baselines inapplicable.
- Name `ACSM 2026`, `WHO 2020`, `ISSN protein`, or `HK CHP` when a material
  recommendation depends on that source.
- State uncertainty instead of inventing a target, nutrient, or causal claim.
